18. Anger

People constantly ask me how they can rid themselves of anger. There's a good reason for this question, as anger is generally viewed as an unpleasant emotion. When expressed in speech or action, anger can have harmful consequences both for the perpetrator and for the victim. Anger is a disruptive mental state that many people would like to do away with. This is completely understandable, since most of us see anger as something negative and unwanted in our lives — like a storm cloud hanging over our heads. Anger can darken our mood and affect our behavior in ways we later regret.

The desire to be free of anger comes from seeing its destructive potential. When we lash out in anger, whether through heated words or aggressive actions, the after-effects can create a widening web of repercussion that touches both the person expressing that anger as well as those on the receiving end. Angry outbursts can damage professional relationships, strain family bonds, and leave lasting emotional scars on the angry person's mind. The social consequences can extend beyond the initial interaction. Expressed anger tends to breed more anger. It creates a contagious atmosphere where heightened emotions can escalate quickly, leading to a harmful sequence of events where rational thought gives way to rude behavior. Understood in this way, a single angry outburst can turn into a chain reaction of hurtful incidents, affecting not just the initial participants but sometimes entire communities.

Anger seldom arises on its own. Like a forest fire that spreads through various types of undergrowth, anger builds and intensifies through the interplay of several other unwholesome mental states — emotions like aversion, ill will, hatred, and resentment — which work together, each supporting and strengthening the others, to produce a continuous cycle of suffering.

Feelings of aversion are often the first stirrings of trouble in the mind. Aversion is experienced as an instinctive turning away from what we find unpleasant or threatening. When we encounter a person or situation that challenges our well-being or sense of security, aversion's immediate rejection creates favorable conditions for more deeply rooted negative states to develop. Rather than observing this discomfort mindfully and then letting it go, we hold on to it, allowing the negativity to open a door to more powerful defilements.

Anger, in contrast, is an energetic and confrontational emotion that moves towards the perceived cause of its discomfort. While aversion recoils from contact, anger pushes forward to meet it head-on. Anger arises when we feel threatened or when we believe our boundaries have been encroached upon. It contains within it the desire to change or even eliminate the source of our displeasure. This is why the Buddha included anger among the dangers to our spiritual progress, because it carries such forceful energy.

As the mind continues to dwell on what is unpleasant, ill will begins to take shape as a result. We start actively wishing for negative outcomes to befall those who have triggered our aversion. We may even find ourselves feeling pleased when adversity or misfortune strikes them. This is a dangerous turning point in the mind's angry reaction. We are no longer simply responding; we are now pursuing harmful intentions toward others.

Anger itself is fundamentally an emotional flashpoint that gives vent to a strong feeling of displeasure or hostility. It can range from mild irritation to intense rage. On its own, anger is neither good nor bad — it's simply a conditioned response, though certainly not a skillful one. Like all conditioned phenomena, it arises dependent on causes and conditions. Understanding this dynamic helps us to observe it with more dispassion.

Ill will, on the other hand, represents a more deliberate attitude. While anger may flare up and subside quickly, ill will persists. It's characterized by a deliberate stance of animosity toward another person. This mindset presents an opportunity for deeper, more tenacious feelings of hatred to take root in the mind. Hatred develops when ill will becomes firmly established. It transforms specific grievances into an overall rejection of another person's right to well-being. Hatred fixes our negative emotions in a rigid framework that strongly resists change or reconciliation. The mind resembles hard, dry wood which is difficult to bend and quick to catch fire. Hatred provides what seems to be a legitimate reason for maintaining our anger, making it feel justified and even righteous.

You should understand that hatred and anger are deeply interconnected, though they operate on different levels of time and awareness. Hatred tends to be a more sustained defilement that can act as both a source and an amplifier of anger. When hatred is an underlying condition in the mind, it lowers the threshold for anger to arise. Because pre-existing hatred has prepared the mind to react with anger, someone harboring hatred becomes angry more quickly and more intensely when encountering the objects of their aversion.

Resentment adds yet another layer of suffering by refusing to let go of past wrongs. It holds on to grievances, keeping them fresh in our minds, not allowing wounds to heal or memories to fade. Resentment constantly rehearses the story of being wronged, ensuring that each new interaction is colored by past pain. This accumulated bitterness becomes readily available fuel for reigniting anger again and again.

Working together, these defilements create what we might call a self-reinforcing cycle of suffering. Aversion prevents us from truly understanding others. Ill will makes us quick to judge them harshly. Hatred convinces us they are inherently flawed and cannot change. Resentment ensures we never forget our grievances. Each mental state feeds the others, making our anger feel increasingly vindicated.

The way out of this tangle, as the Buddha taught, begins with wise attention: clearly seeing these states as they arise in the mind. By observing anger and its related defilements with detachment, we begin to understand their conditioned nature. They are not "self" or "mine" but merely passing phenomena arising from various causes and conditions.

In our relationships, anger often acts like a wedge pushing away the people we care about. We might speak hurtful words in the heat of the moment that damage trust and create lasting rifts. In social situations, unchecked anger can harm our reputation and interfere with achieving our goals. Perhaps most importantly, chronic anger can trap us in a mood of negative thinking, making it harder for us to find joy or maintain our perspective in challenging situations. It can drain our emotional energy and prevent us from addressing the underlying issues that triggered anger in the first place. This is why developing strategies to deal with anger is so important. Understanding this dynamic is crucial for anyone seeking to manage their anger more effectively and to help others do the same. Only by recognizing how these emotional states feed into each other can we begin to dismantle the framework of anger and develop healthier forms of emotional response.

Standing as a hindrance to that effort is the fact that many people feel they have a right to be angry, and because of that they believe that their anger is not really wrong. They justify their anger by blaming others for intentionally doing things to make them angry. "It is always someone else's fault — don't blame me," they say. The problem is, when people act out their angry emotions, they are likely to receive an angry response in return. Even if they feel completely justified in their anger, expressing it usually creates more conflict and pain and does nothing to solve the original problem. On the other hand, calmly observing others' behavior with mindful awareness instead of immediate judgment or anger can lead to better relationships and less suffering for everyone involved.

Even though we think we have good reasons for communicating our displeasure, all anger directed at others — no matter how justified or how righteous it may appear — originates from unwholesome feelings of aversion and hatred. Whether angry actions are expressed in violent ways or nonviolent ways, the effect is still harmful, and displays of anger will never bring about circumstances leading to forgiveness and reconciliation.

We can always find some excuse for our anger and a reason to point the finger at someone else. The issue, however, isn't what other people do to us. The real issue is the anger which exists within our own hearts, just waiting for a pretext to express itself — and of course, some excuse will always turn up. An angry emotion is always harmful, no matter how we defend it. So it's the causes of the harmful emotions within our hearts that we must seek to remedy.

Anger and irritation tend to arise in the mind when we are faced with someone who we feel is unpleasant or threatening. This perception appears to give us good cause to resent that person. But let's look at it from a different angle. When we suffer the physical pain of sickness, we know very well that the pain is due to bodily disturbances. Although we dislike the pain, it would be silly to angrily resent that pain or the body that produced it. Similarly, when people act unkindly, it's because their minds are troubled. They're caught up in their own confusion and struggling with their own problems. They are behaving under the influence of their mental defilements. Why should we resent people who are victims of their own deluded thinking? Shouldn't we feel sorry for them instead? While dealing with unpleasant behavior isn't easy, understanding why people act badly can help us cope better. Remember that when someone acts with bad intentions, they're creating their own future problems. Given this, what good does our anger serve?

Pride and conceit are the hindrances that prevent most people from recognizing this truth. To better understand it, people need to let go of their self-importance. This means accepting that they are not as special or different as they think they are. It means realizing that the boundaries between us and others are not as clear-cut as we imagine. Most people find this idea uncomfortable. They resist it because accepting this truth would require them to change how they live. They would need to give up many of their customary behaviors and motivations. When people truly accept that they share a close relationship with the people in their lives, they cannot reasonably maintain hatred towards any of them, because due to their interrelatedness, hating others would be equivalent to hating themselves.

To understand how to prevent anger from arising in the mind, we must become aware of our own emotions and how they affect the motives that underlie our interactions with other people. Basically, there are two opposite emotional attitudes: one reacts with dislike towards other people and our relationships with them, and the other reacts favorably towards others and our relationships with them. To better understand these reactions, we should investigate within ourselves to see where these feelings come from and what's really taking place when we interact with others.

When we carefully investigate our reactions to the behavior of others, we find that at their most fundamental level they are triggered by sensory impressions: vibrations of air molecules reaching our ears that we label as praise or criticism; patterns of light and shadow falling upon our retinas that we interpret as friendly or hostile gestures; pressure and temperature sensations on our skin that we classify as gentle or aggressive physical contact. Even when we are beaten or kicked, on that same fundamental level it is no more than physical matter encountering physical matter. At their most basic, these experiences are just our senses of touch, sight, and hearing picking up information from the world around us. The meanings and feelings we attach to these experiences — whether we see them as pleasant or unpleasant — are created by our minds. Which means that our quick judgments about liking or disliking people are not based on any solid reality. They are simply based on our emotional interpretations of sense data.

If we understand this, we can start treating people more fairly and objectively, without automatically sorting them into categories of those who we like and those who we dislike. By seeing through such emotional reactions, we can develop a more balanced and calm approach to our relationships with others and stop being controlled by our spur-of-the-moment emotional responses. Looking at people without judging them helps us better understand why they behave the way they do. For the most part, people become mean, jealous, or violent because they're driven by self-interest and they fail to see how those emotions control them. By stepping back and observing angry people, we can see that their harmful actions often stem from being unaware of themselves and their moods. People become hateful or cruel because they're trapped in their own confusion, as though they're lost in a mental fog. They doggedly hold tightly to their urges and beliefs without realizing how this hurts both themselves and others.

It is by making distinctions that we define ourselves. We like hating some people and loving other people because that defines who we are as opposed to them. We like experiencing strong emotions because it defines us as distinct from the target of our emotional outbursts. We prefer viewing people as enemies because it helps us feel more certain about who we are.

Our adversaries are generally of two types: those that are hot-tempered and hostile by nature, and those that are usually friendly but occasionally lose their temper. We tend to get upset at both types, but is this reasonable? Just as we don't get mad at fire for being hot, we shouldn't get mad at someone who is hot-tempered by nature. Similarly, getting angry at those who have temporarily lost their temper is like being angry at the weather because the sun is hot. When people act aggressively, it's because their minds are overwhelmed by strong emotions. Instead of being angry at a person, it makes more sense to recognize that the harmful emotion itself is the real problem.

When two people are in conflict, they often fall into roles that seem to be in opposition. One person appears to be the aggressor while the other is seen as the victim of aggression. However, these two people are actually bound together in a relationship where each person's actions affect the other. The aggressor might depend on the victim's reactions to feel powerful, while the victim might prolong the situation through their aversion to the aggression. This connection between them makes it harder for either person to simply walk away from the confrontation. Their behaviors and responses become connected, creating a downward cycle that can be difficult to break. Most experiences like that are conditioned by kamma. Events that seem beyond our control are often the consequences of our former actions, which means that it is not entirely correct to blame someone else for our suffering. To some extent, the effects of our own past actions attract the hurtful responses we endure.

When people display anger, they are often engaged in a form of fishing. They are casting out their anger like a baited hook in the hope that someone will take it. This isn't a spontaneous response but rather a calculated action. The person expressing anger is attempting to provoke you into responding with your own anger. If you succumb to this provocation and become angry yourself, you fulfill precisely what they intended. Now both sides can justify their anger. Your heated response becomes the proof they needed that their anger was justified, and so the drama escalates. This back-and-forth exchange of angry retorts creates a field day for the defilements.

This behavior reveals a lot about the roots of human anger. Some individuals crave constant attention, behaving like children who cry out to be noticed. Others enjoy an illusory sense of power by manipulating the emotions of those around them. Many people engage in elaborate narratives to justify their anger and resentment, and some simply find ordinary life so boring that they decide to stir up trouble.

When confronting difficult individuals, you may experience a profound resentment burning within your heart. You might find yourself pacing about for hours afterward, consumed by the pain of your wounded feelings while fighting against the anger spiraling within your heart that feels like a coiled snake ready to strike. All the while you are struggling to restrain your emotions and regain composure. This internal struggle is necessary because the principle of right action demands that self-restraint win this battle. In emotionally challenging encounters, merely controlling your speech feels like trying to hold back a flood with your bare hands. However, the longer you persist in practicing self-discipline, the more you will come to realize that these troublesome people have entered your life for a good purpose — albeit one wrapped up with thorns.

When someone hurls angry words at you, where do these words actually land? Can they touch the essence of your being? Can they damage the knowing quality within your mind? Examine this deeply during your meditation practice. You'll eventually see that their anger is like mud thrown at the sky. The mud falls harmlessly to the ground while the sky remains untouched and unblemished. It is only through your own attachment — through taking these words as personal attacks — that suffering can find a foothold within your heart.

As your understanding deepens, you'll come to see that such difficult people are not really your enemies. They are actually imprisoned by their own mental defilements, creating seeds of future suffering with each angry outburst. How could you maintain resentment toward someone in such a trapped condition? That would be like resenting the behavior of a person burning with fever who in his delirium lashes out at those trying to help.

In time, you will come to view these difficult people as your teachers. By provoking your anger, they force you to develop qualities that peaceful meditation alone couldn't achieve: patience as immovable as a mountain, determination as unyielding as steel, and wisdom that penetrates through the illusion of self-righteousness. These challenges appear in your life for a purpose, showing you that your real enemies don't exist out there but are dwelling inside your own heart.

If we can maintain our composure when people hurt us with words or actions, we can overcome two adversaries at the same time. The first adversary is the individual inflicting the pain. The second — and perhaps more formidable one — is our own rising anger. With this understanding, we begin to perceive our antagonists in a new light. Rather than seeing them as opponents to be defeated, we recognize them as teachers offering valuable lessons. In this sense, we should welcome these difficult people as allies on our spiritual journey. They assist us in ways that our friends and family simply cannot.

Once we've learned to corral our anger, we find beneath its troubled surface a potent energy. This force, when cleansed of its harmful aspects, can drive significant transformation in our lives. As anger arises in our minds, we feel an undeniable intensity moving through us — a strong impulse towards action. If we can separate this raw energy from its unwholesome companions like ill will, hatred, and resentment, we discover that this force contains the power to bring about meaningful change. This positive force, when channeled toward wholesome ends, can become fuel for our meditation practice rather than an obstacle to our progress.

When positive anger arises, it gives the mind a complete and unyielding focus on its mission. The mind refuses to let go of its commitment or compromise its principles in any way. When cleansed of defilements and properly directed toward Dhamma practice, this powerful quality becomes what the Buddha referred to as ādhipateyya — an unwavering resolve which can overcome all barriers on the path.

This is especially true for those who have what the Buddha called a dosa-carita temperament — that is, people with sharp, discriminating minds naturally inclined toward criticism and finding fault. Although this critical faculty may manifest as anger, irritation, or even hatred when directed outward at others, it becomes transformative when turned inward through dedicated Dhamma practice. The dosa person's mind resembles a knife in that it swiftly cuts through entangling emotions. When properly directed toward our own defilements rather than toward others' shortcomings, this sharpness enables us to catch ourselves in the act of thinking or behaving unskillfully. The very energy that would be used to criticize others can then be directed inward and become the means of seeing our defilements clearly and correcting our own faults.

Looking at anger through the lens of mindful attention reveals that it is not just a single emotion but rather a complex experience with many layers. The real challenge isn't to suppress its powerful energy but to skillfully redirect it. When we examine anger closely, we can separate its harmful elements — the desire to inflict pain, the smoldering resentment, and the vengeful thoughts — from the fundamental energy at its core. This underlying force, when properly channeled, can become a powerful support for our highest aspirations.

By transforming rather than thwarting this energy, we convert an emotion which could cause suffering into a force that can serve our spiritual growth. This transformation demonstrates what the Buddha described as the middle way: we neither give free rein to anger through uncontrolled expression nor do we repress it through avoidance. Instead, we meet anger with wise attention, comprehending its true nature and redirecting its essential force towards Dhamma practice. The very energy that once drove conflicts or harmful behaviors becomes the powerful current that moves us towards skillful action.

Perhaps most remarkably, this practice reveals that following the Buddha's Noble Path doesn't require perfect circumstances or the absence of difficulties. Rather, it invites us to work with life exactly as it is, so that we can convert these difficulties into aids on the path to enlightenment — not by eliminating them but by changing how we relate to them. Through persistent practice, even our troublesome emotions become valued guides on the path, steering us towards enlightenment and freedom from all animosity.
