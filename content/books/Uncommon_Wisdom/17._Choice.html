17. Choice

It's important for Buddhist practitioners to understand that no matter how challenging their life situation happens to be, they can still practice the way of Dhamma. The circumstances of a person's life need not be an obstacle to Dhamma practice. Whether conditions are favorable or unfavorable is less relevant than your attitude towards the practice itself.

The key is to recognize that Dhamma practice isn't limited to formal meditation. When you perform your daily work with mindfulness and careful attention to detail — be it washing dishes, driving to work, or working a job — you are actively training the mind to pay attention. In that way, each activity becomes an opportunity to develop mindful awareness. Even unpleasant circumstances can be used to develop patience and equanimity. Life's circumstances themselves are neutral; it is your response to them that determines whether they aid or hinder the development of understanding. A proper attitude will transform all activities into valid Dhamma practice.

If you maintain this attitude in the activities of daily life, when you do have a chance to practice formal meditation, you'll find that you've already developed a proper foundation. The mind will settle readily into meditation because you've been training it steadily day by day.

Don't think that living as a lay person prevents you from walking the path of Dhamma. Though it may not offer the favorable conditions of a meditation retreat, it certainly provides ample opportunity for serious practice. Your task is to work with circumstances as they present themselves, finding creative ways to turn them toward the Dhamma. If you apply yourself with sincerity, a way forward will open for you. Keep in mind that mindfulness, wisdom, and persistence are three essential qualities that you'll need to firmly establish that momentum. Keep cultivating them, and you'll find that your whole life becomes a form of meditation practice, regardless of the outer conditions you face. Everything you do can be transformed into meditation if you approach it with the right mindset.

But keep in mind that a tendency to always choose options that promote comfort and convenience shapes life circumstances in profound ways. When you become accustomed to indulging in comfort, even minor discomforts are magnified, making them appear more challenging and difficult than they truly are. On the reverse side, simplicity in your choices leads to feelings of freedom. When you learn to live embracing just your essential needs with minimal consideration for comfort, you discover then that your practice can flourish anywhere under any circumstances, as it's no longer dependent on external conditions.

Regrettably, modern society has glorified choice, treating it as if it were the very essence of freedom. People are caught up in the belief that happiness comes from having endless options in what they buy, what they do, or how they live. They chase after pleasurable pursuits, convinced that by following their desires and personal preferences they will find satisfaction. This belief is a dangerous delusion. While the need to make choices is part of human life, attachment to choice becomes a subtle form of bondage.

When we examine this closely, we see that having more choices often creates mental agitation rather than peace of mind. The mind exhausts itself trying to weigh countless options, always wondering if the right decision was made, always fearing something better was overlooked. Consider how much time and energy is wasted in life just deciding between trivial pursuits: which brand to buy, which entertainment to watch, which food to eat. This constant decision-making drains our mental resources and creates unnecessary anxiety. The more choices we face, the more we become enslaved to the very process of choosing.

Some argue that we've progressed beyond any need to honor traditional views about the value of restraint and moderation. They point to societies where age-old moral guidelines have been replaced by a liberating license to indulge themselves. But look more carefully at the results this attitude has produced. People in these societies often lack moral stability and inner peace. Without clear principles to guide them, they drift aimlessly in a sea of choices, unable to find real purpose or meaning in their lives.

Of course, genuine freedom does not come from the possibility of unlimited options. It comes from understanding the debilitating effects of unrestrained desire and attachment. When we understand deeply how addiction to choosing creates stress and confusion, we naturally move toward a simpler, more sober way of living. This opens the path to true freedom — one not attained through a multiplication of choices but through wisdom that sees beyond the compulsion to indulge.

In the end, what appears to be freedom in our consumer culture is often just an agreeable form of imprisonment. People have gained the ability to choose whatever they want but have lost the wisdom to know which choices are truly worthwhile. They mistake variety for happiness, failing to see how their attachment to choice keeps them strapped to the yoke of suffering.

To protect ourselves in this age of material abundance, we must examine how choices affect the mind. What we call free choice is often nothing more than the mind responding to the urges of craving and clinging. People become enslaved by their own preferences, constructing elaborate lifestyles that reflect and reinforce their attachments. They surround themselves with objects and experiences that mirror their desires, creating what they believe to be a unique personal identity. But this identity is nothing more than an accumulation of preferences and aversions, likes and dislikes — all of which are constantly changing and ultimately unsatisfying.

Consider how society operates: everywhere we look, there are shops and advertisements providing endless options. Each option promises happiness, each claims to fulfill our desires. Meanwhile, the marketplace has become extraordinarily sophisticated in its understanding of human weakness, as it presents us with numerous variations of the same items — countless models of cars, infinite styles of clothing, and endless forms of entertainment. This variety is styled as a cherished freedom, but is it? What really happens is that people become exhausted by the range of choices offered to them, as they spend their precious human birth weighing the merits of frivolous options. This is how craving works: like a dog chasing its own tail, the choosing mind runs in circles, always believing that the next choice will bring contentment. But contentment cannot arise from simply choosing between options. Instead, it comes from understanding the addictive allure of having multiple choices — even if most of them are unrealistic.

This perpetual mismatch between desire and reality is precisely what the Buddha identified as a fundamental source of human suffering. The nature of craving creates a gap between what is possible and what we wish were possible. When we get the things we don't want, we suffer from aversion. When we don't get the things we do want, we suffer from unfulfilled desire. Even when we do get what we want, we often find it unsatisfying, or we worry about losing it. Often what we desperately wanted yesterday becomes meaningless today, and what we once avoided might later become precious to us. This shifting nature of desire reveals its fickle and unpredictable character.

The Lord Buddha showed us a different way. Instead of constantly reaching outward to find contentment, we can learn to rest calmly with inner awareness. When we sit quietly and simply stay with the breath, what happens? At first the mind rebels. It wants entertainment, stimulation, something to grasp at. But if we patiently persist at limiting our attention to just this one simple object, the mind gradually settles down and becomes quiet. This is why the Buddha established clear guidelines for practice that show us how to focus our attention effectively. It's like digging a well: if we dig a hundred shallow holes, we'll never find water; but if we dig one hole deep enough, eventually we'll reach what we're seeking. In this way, the restrictions undertaken in meditation practice aren't walls to imprison us; rather, they're the sides of the well that allow us to confidently dig to the depth of clear comprehension.

The deeper we investigate, the more we'll see that what society calls freedom is actually a subtle form of bondage. Every choice we make generates attachment that creates kamma, thus producing the conditions for our future suffering. By their very nature, our choices cannot provide the peace of mind we crave. The more intensely we want something, the more we suffer from its absence. The more we reject our present circumstances, the more we suffer from their presence in our lives.

Despite this harmful tendency, we must understand that choice itself is not the cause of the problem. The Buddha taught that right intention is an important factor on the Noble Eightfold Path. This critical factor represents the quality of mind behind our choices. Are we choosing based on wisdom or based on delusion? Are our choices leading us in the direction of freedom or toward further entanglement?

When we choose with wisdom, we naturally incline toward simplicity at the expense of complexity, toward calmness rather than stimulation. We begin to see that true freedom doesn't come from having unlimited options but from understanding the fickle nature of the desires that grasp at the choices. This insight allows us to make choices that will support our spiritual development.

That development involves cultivating mindfulness and clear comprehension in our decision-making. Before deciding on an option, we should contemplate: will this choice lead to more craving or less? Will it create more complexity in my life or more simplicity? Will it support my meditation practice or hinder it? This kind of investigation helps us to use our ability to choose wisely, directing it toward what is truly beneficial.

In the end, the highest choice we can make is to choose the path to enlightenment, which focuses our attention towards following the Noble Eightfold Path to a clear understanding of the Four Noble Truths. This is the choice that leads to indisputable happiness. All other choices should be evaluated in light of whether they support or obstruct this fundamental intention. This is what the Lord Buddha meant when he spoke of yoniso manasikāra, or wise reflection. Through such reflection, we can use our ability to choose skillfully, transforming it from a source of confusion and disappointment into a tool for seeking the liberating truths of the Buddha's teaching.

So what is the root cause of our suffering? Look carefully, and you will see it stems from the mind's habit of grasping and clinging. The mind moves restlessly from one desire to another, always craving, always thirsting for something new. And what helps to fuel this endless wanting? It is the sheer abundance of choices that life presents us with.

There is, however, a way to break free from this obsession. It begins with a simple but profound shift: deliberately choosing to live with fewer wants and needs. This attitude is not just about stoic abstinence or deprivation. It's more about the wise recognition that the door to true peace opens only when we stop agitating the mind with bouts of unrestrained craving. When we create an environment of simplicity in our lives, the mind — no longer provoked by nagging desires — begins to settle into a natural state of clarity. In this clarity, we can savor the taste of freedom from wanting itself. Here begins the path to genuine contentment, which points us towards what truly matters for our spiritual well-being.

This attitude encompasses the essence of true renunciation. This renunciation is not simply a dreary denial of life's pleasures; it is the skillful use of our wisdom to recognize what really brings happiness into our lives. When we see clearly how following the dictates of craving leads only to more and more desires, and realize that all we cling to is inherently unstable, we will be increasingly inclined to let go of those desires. When we truly understand the unreliability of worldly pleasures, we naturally turn toward what is reliable: the peace of a mind freed from wanting. This is not a teaching of despair but rather one of hope, for in releasing our grip on what cannot last, we open ourselves to a happiness that will endure.

The practice of renunciation requires patient effort. Old mental habits do not disappear overnight. But through persistent contemplation of these truths, combined with practical steps to simplify our lives, we gradually experience the relief that comes with wanting less.

When we step onto the path of renunciation, this choice carries with it full responsibility for our reactions to whatever happens as a result. Practitioners may suppose that acts of renunciation will bring immediate positive results, but what it actually brings first is a stark confrontation with our own addictive mental habits. Consider what happens when you deliberately limit your choices. The mind, accustomed to having its way, begins to struggle. It's like a wild horse that's been cornered; at first it kicks and bucks, showing us exactly how untamed it is. But seeing this reaction clearly is precisely the value of renunciation. It shines a light on the patterns of reaction the mind has built up over the course of our lives.

The success or failure of our practice hinges on how we handle these reactions. When discomfort arises — as it surely will — do we immediately try to escape, or do we patiently watch and learn? This is where real practice begins. Each moment of voluntary restriction opens another laboratory for investigating the mind and its perceptions.

In meditation especially, this principle becomes abundantly clear. When practicing meditation, unconstrained choice is a curse. It inevitably leads more towards mental discord than towards peace of mind. The restrictions required in meditation actually help to concentrate attention and encourage a unity of purpose. In fact, we can increase our capacity for mindfulness by reducing our options to just sitting alone in one posture, focusing on one meditation object. This can free the mind of distraction and foster a stable inner balance.

The Buddha emphasized moral discipline as a foundation for meditation because it requires us to live by ethical principles that eliminate countless unskillful choices in daily life. Intention underlies moral behavior, wherein choices are determined by considering codes of moral conduct such as the Buddhist precepts. The reasoning behind these precepts is that we all have the ability and the freedom to select right over wrong, and therefore can deliberately choose to act appropriately in all situations.

One of the functions of monastic communities, with their rules of conduct and training practices, is to limit choice. While living in a Buddhist monastery, for instance, our freedom is influenced by the perspectives of the community. This constraint is both protective — in that wayward choices are discouraged — and liberating — in that wise choices are encouraged. A collective morality is cultivated whereby principles of sense restraint take precedence over self-indulgence, and social cohesion holds sway over individual preferences.

People who practice the relatively strict discipline at a Buddhist monastery often come away feeling rejuvenated and refreshed precisely because their choices were restricted. They feel at ease and alive in a way that they did not in their everyday lives. They discover that by restricting their range of activities to just meditation and other basic responsibilities, they are able to experience a freedom they never suspected possible.

What is the nature of this freedom? It is the experience of not being ruled by habitual emotional responses when confronted with conflicting choices. When the mind is lucid and unaffected, it ceases to be the victim of mental confusion. It sees situations more clearly, realizing that out of countless possibilities, there is usually only one or possibly two courses of action that are truly worth pursuing. Freedom from the burden of impulsive reactions leaves us with little else to do but take the direction that life offers us in each moment, to pursue a contentment that transcends mundane happiness.

The profound truth is that real choice — the kind that leads to transcendence — only becomes possible when the mind is steady and clear. This clarity arises precisely because we've chosen to restrict our options. It's like focusing a scattered beam of light through a lens. The very act of constraining it creates power and precision.

Remember, the purpose of all this isn't just to become a person who can endure hardship. The purpose is to discover that genuine peace and happiness don't depend on external conditions at all. This is what the Lord Buddha meant when he spoke about going against the stream. We renounce not to gain something but to discover what's already there when we stop chasing after sense pleasures. When you understand this, you'll see that renunciation isn't a burden — it's a blessing. It's the very thing that allows us to see the truth of our situation clearly, and in that clarity lies the introduction to genuine freedom.

I remember when I first came to Thailand from England. I had left behind a world full of choices — careers, possessions, entertainments, and so on. At first, the simplicity of monastery life seemed restrictive. But gradually I discovered that as external choices fell away, internal understanding grew stronger. I began to notice this in my meditation practice as well. In the early days, I struggled with the monastery schedule, the limited food, and just the bare necessities. But gradually I saw how those restrictions were actually protecting something valuable: the space to develop calm and concentration. I remember one time when sitting in my kuṭi after the morning meal, I realized that I felt more genuinely free than I ever had in my supposedly free life in England.

There's a profound truth here. The more we chase after what we believe freedom means — endless choices, constant stimulation, unlimited options — the more we become enslaved by our own wanting mind. I've observed this directly in my years at the monastery. Newcomers often arrive full of concerns about all the things they'll have to give up. Within months, many laugh at their former worries. Why? Because they've discovered something quite practical: the more you pare away what's unnecessary, the more energy you have for what matters. It's really quite simple.

I've also noticed how Western visitors often resist the monastery's schedule, believing that following it strictly curtails their cherished freedoms. But watching closely, I could see their actions weren't free at all. They were actually driven by deeply ingrained habits, by what felt comfortable to them, and by the pleasures they were used to. True freedom, as the Buddha taught, comes from seeing through such restrictive tendencies.

This reminds me of a Western monk who came to the monastery. He had been a successful businessman with a large house, fancy car, and expensive holidays. He thought giving these up would be a great sacrifice. But after some time in the simple monastery environment, he told me: "For the first time I feel truly free. Before, I was always a slave to maintaining all those possessions."

Now consider the forest monk who owns nothing but his eight basic requisites. Does he spend his time fretting over what to wear, what to eat, what kind of roof he has over his head? No. His mind is free to pursue the real work of meditation. His simplicity isn't poverty; it's wealth of a different kind.

Restraint is not merely a restriction; it is a skillful means of protecting the mind. Consider how the average person faced with endless choices becomes worn out by pursuing the very freedom they cherish. Their minds constantly jump this way and that until they become fatigued by the constant need to decide, to choose, to satisfy competing desires. When living in the monastery, however, these burdens tend to gradually fall away.

Over the years I've observed countless people who come here to learn about Buddhist practice. Initially they might balk at the monastic strictness — one meal a day, no entertainment, hours of seated meditation. Yet invariably, as the days pass, their faces brighten and their movements become more composed. Why is that? Because they've been relieved of the heavy burden of choice.

Some of the daily choices that we make have so much power that the ripple effects they cause will eventually touch many other people. The decisions we make and the actions they lead to are part of what creates the moral environment we all must live in. Whether we choose to speak kindly or harshly, to act with mindfulness or negligence, to give or to withhold — these seemingly small decisions shape not only our own kamma but they weave themselves into the fabric of society as a whole.

Consider how a single act of kindness or harshness can influence another person's state of mind, leading them to carry that same mental attitude into their next encounter. In this way, our choices flow from person to person, creating currents in the sea of human experience. One person's patience may give rise to another's peace of mind; one person's anger may kindle another's fear. The moral environment we live in is not created by governments or institutions alone, but by the accumulated weight of countless individual choices — yours and mine.

When we truly understand this, we see that each moment holds the seed of transformation. Whether that seed grows into wholesome or unwholesome fruit depends on the strength of our mindfulness and wisdom at each moment of decision. This understanding places great responsibility in our hands, but it also gives us great power to affect positive change. By cultivating awareness of our choices and their far-reaching effects, we can contribute to creating an environment that supports the growth of wisdom and compassion for all living beings.

The challenge then isn't to maximize choice but to optimize it — to cultivate mindsets that support rather than overwhelm our decision-making capabilities. This might mean deliberately limiting our options in certain areas of life so we can engage more meaningfully with the choices that truly matter. In doing so, we might find that the freest life isn't the one with the most options, but the one with the best constraints.
